<Analogy title="A ramp next to the stairs">
A ramp at a building's entrance was built for wheelchair users. But it also helps parents with prams, delivery people with trolleys and anyone with a sore knee. Building for people with disabilities usually makes things better for **everyone**.
</Analogy>

## What accessibility means

<Term word="accessibility">Accessibility</Term> (often written "a11y": an "a", then 11 letters, then a "y") means making pages that everyone can use, including people who:

- are blind or have low vision, and use a <Term word="screen-reader">screen reader</Term> or zoom in a lot
- are colour-blind (about 1 in 12 men)
- can't use a mouse, and move around with the keyboard (Tab key) instead
- are deaf, and need captions on videos

Good news: most of it comes free with **good HTML**. A real `button` can be reached with the Tab key and pressed with Enter. A `div` made to look like a button can't, unless you do a lot of extra work.

## Writing good alt text

Ask: "If I described this picture to a friend on the phone, what would I say?"

- Weak: `alt="image"` or `alt="photo1.jpg"`. These tell the listener nothing.
- Better: `alt="Dog"`.
- Good: `alt="A brown dog catching a frisbee on Clifton beach"`.

Don't start with "Image of…". The screen reader already says it's an image.

**Decorative pictures**, like a pretty divider line, add nothing for a listener. Give them **empty** alt text, `alt=""`, and the screen reader skips them. Empty is different from missing: with no alt at all, some screen readers read out the file name instead.

## Five habits to keep

1. **Use the right element**: `button` for buttons, `a` for links, headings in order.
2. **Label every input** (you did this in the last unit).
3. **Write alt text** for every meaningful image.
4. **Don't rely on colour alone.** "Fields in red are required" fails for colour-blind people. Add a word or a symbol too.
5. **Make link words describe the destination**, never just "click here".

## Try it on your Mac

Press **⌘F5** to turn on VoiceOver, the Mac's built-in screen reader. Visit any website and listen for a minute, then press **⌘F5** again to turn it off. Notice how much it depends on headings, labels and alt text.

<KeyIdea>
Accessibility means pages that everyone can use. Most of it comes from **good HTML**: the right elements, labelled inputs, descriptive alt text (or `alt=""` for decoration), and never using colour alone.
</KeyIdea>
