<Analogy title="Postcards and sealed envelopes">
A postcard can be read by every person who handles it on the way. A sealed, tamper-proof envelope can't. Plain HTTP is a postcard. <Term word="https">HTTPS</Term> is a sealed envelope.
</Analogy>

## What HTTPS does

Data hops through many computers on its way: your Wi-Fi router, your internet provider, undersea cables. With plain <Term word="http">HTTP</Term>, any of them could read or change it, including passwords.

HTTPS uses <Term word="encryption">encryption</Term> to scramble everything between your browser and the server. Only those two can unscramble it. It also checks the server really owns the domain name, using a digital certificate.

That's what the padlock icon in the address bar means.

## What the padlock does NOT mean

The padlock means **the connection is private**. It does **not** mean the website is honest. Scam sites can get padlocks too, for free. Always check the **domain name** as well.

## For developers

- Every site you build should use HTTPS. Browsers warn people away from sites that don't.
- When you deploy in Trail 10, hosting services set up HTTPS for you automatically and for free.
- `localhost` (your own Mac) is the exception: the data never leaves your computer, so plain HTTP is fine there.

<KeyIdea>
**HTTPS** encrypts the conversation so nobody in between can read or change it. The padlock proves the connection is **private**, not that the site is **trustworthy**.
</KeyIdea>
