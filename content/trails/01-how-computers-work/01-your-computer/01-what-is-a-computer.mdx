<Analogy title="A very fast, very obedient cook">
Picture a cook who has never had an original idea in their life, but who can follow a recipe perfectly, a billion steps a second, without ever getting tired. They do **exactly** what the recipe says. Even when the recipe is wrong.

That cook is a computer.
</Analogy>

## Input, processing, output

Whatever the computer is (your MacBook, your phone, a bank's machine in a data centre), it does the same three things over and over:

1. **Input**: it receives information. You type a key, tap the screen, or a message arrives over the internet.
2. **Processing**: it follows instructions to do something with that information. It adds numbers, sorts a list, or decides which picture to show.
3. **Output**: it shows or sends a result. Letters appear on screen, a sound plays, or a message is sent.

When you type a WhatsApp message and press send: the keyboard is **input**, the phone turning your words into data and packaging it up is **processing**, and the message leaving your phone is **output**.

## Memory and storage

A computer also needs somewhere to keep things.

- <Term word="memory">**Memory** (also called RAM)</Term> is like the cook's countertop. It's fast, and it holds what's being worked on right now. It's cleared when the power goes off.
- <Term word="storage">**Storage**</Term> (your Mac's SSD, the "disk") is like the pantry. It's slower to reach, but things stay there even when the computer is switched off. Your photos and documents live here.

<KeyIdea>
A computer is a machine that **follows instructions** to turn **input** into **output**. It's fast and precise, but not clever. It only does what it's told.
</KeyIdea>

That last point will matter a lot later. When your code does something odd, the computer isn't being stubborn. It's following your instructions exactly. Finding the instruction that says something different from what you meant is most of what programming is.
